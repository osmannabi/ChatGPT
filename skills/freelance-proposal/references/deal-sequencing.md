# Deal sequencing: why good proposals go quiet

Source: a qualitative review of 30 archived Fiverr conversations (15 August 2026), each read
alongside the proposal sent, the buyer's subsequent messages, and the terminal outcome. It
supplements an earlier inbox review and reaches the same conclusion.

Read this whenever a brief looks big, vague, or early; whenever a buyer objects to price; and
whenever Mehmet comes back with a buyer's reply to a qualifying message.

**Caveat worth carrying:** this is pattern analysis, not a controlled study, and archived threads
over-represent non-conversions by definition. Treat it as a strong prior, not a law. Fiverr matching
and admin threads were excluded and should stay excluded from any conversion counting.

---

## The finding

Reply and acceptance are driven by **decision readiness and fit**, not by proposal length or
polish. Long, well-crafted proposals repeatedly became the last message in the thread when they
arrived before the buyer had confirmed scope, budget, technical constraints, or a low-risk first
step.

The proposals that died were not weak. They contained deliverable systems, workflow detail, revision
counts, technical reasoning, portfolio links, and thorough discovery questions. That is exactly the
problem: a buyer who is still deciding has to process an entire project before they can take any
next step, so they take none.

## The pilot ladder

The fix is to propose the smallest credible first stage with a fixed price and an objective
deliverable, then name the rollout only after it is accepted. It converts a difficult yes/no into a
bounded one, and it is honest: the first piece genuinely carries the setup cost.

| Job type | First stage | Rough band |
|---|---|---|
| Product / CPG retouch or compositing, multi-image | One hero image, fully finished, to lock the look | $150 – $400 |
| 3D / CGI product, multi-SKU | One product build plus an approved lighting test | $400 – $900 |
| Amazon listing set | One main image plus one infographic | $120 – $250 |
| AI imagery, experimental or unproven look | One proof-of-concept image, or a 3-image style test | $80 – $250 |
| AI video | One 5-second test clip at the target look | $150 – $400 |
| Packaging / label design, range | One label concept for a single SKU | $400 – $900 |
| Campaign / brand imagery | One key visual, before adaptations | $400 – $1,200 |
| Workflow setup (Weave / ComfyUI) | A working node graph for the single hardest step | $150 – $300 |

Phrase it as a decision, not a discount:

> "I'd start by building one approved [hero visual / product template / test clip] so we lock the
> quality and the workflow before applying it across the full set. That first stage is $X, delivered
> by [date]. Once it's approved I'll use the same system for the remaining [quantity]."

Use a pilot especially for experimental AI work, high-volume campaigns, and anything technically
unfamiliar. Those are where a buyer's risk is highest and a fixed small step is worth most.

## The recommended sequence

1. **Mirror the buyer's desired outcome** in one sentence.
2. **Confirm one deal-critical constraint**: budget, deadline, platform, quantity, or existing assets.
3. **Propose the smallest credible first stage** with a fixed price and an objective deliverable.
4. **Name the rollout path** only after the first stage is accepted.
5. **Follow up once** after 4-7 days, tailored, with an opt-out and a lower-scope alternative.

### Example first response

> Based on [specific reference or goal], I'd start by creating one approved [hero visual / template /
> test clip] so we lock the quality and workflow before applying it across the full set. That first
> stage would be $X, delivered by [date]. If it's approved I'll use the same system for the remaining
> [quantity]. Do you already have a target budget for the rollout, or should I price the first stage
> only?

## Handling the reply

### They gave a budget band
Quote inside it, staged, first stage priced separately. If the band genuinely can't carry the work,
go to the three paths below rather than quietly cutting quality.

### They said it's too expensive
Price objections are usually a real constraint, not a negotiating move. Explaining *why* the work
costs what it costs does not give them anything to buy. Offer three explicit paths:

1. **Full scope** — the original price and outcome.
2. **Starter scope** — one concrete component at a lower price.
3. **Advice or handoff** — a paid consultation or a production specification if they'll execute it
   elsewhere.

### They want proof before committing
Do not debate the request; the concern is reasonable even when the proposed solution isn't workable.
Offer a safe paid alternative:

> "I can't produce the final work before an order, but I can set up a small paid proof stage: a
> character reference sheet / one low-resolution concept. You approve the likeness and direction
> before the full composition stage begins."

### They need something Mehmet doesn't do
Say it before quoting, and pair it with the adjacent thing he can do:

> "Before I quote, one requirement to confirm: I work in [tool/process] and deliver [output]. If you
> need [specific alternative], I'm not the right production partner for that part, though I can help
> with [adjacent deliverable]."

### They asked directly for a fixed price
Give one, for a defined stage. Answering a price question with more questions reads as evasion and
recurs in the stalled threads.

## Follow-ups

The message that failed, repeatedly, was: *"Hi, just checking in regarding my previous offer… short
call?"* It names nothing specific, reduces no risk, offers no new option, and makes no decision
easier. It was the terminal outbound in a large share of archived threads.

A follow-up has to carry something new. Send once, 4-7 days out, and always with a clean way to say
no; giving someone permission to decline is what makes them answer at all.

> "You mentioned the project needed [specific outcome]. I can reserve the smaller [pilot/template]
> option at $X and deliver it by [date]. Should I keep that open, or close the quote for now?"

> "Before I close this, would the blocker be budget, timing, or scope? If it helps, I can reduce this
> to [specific reduced deliverable] rather than the full package."

> "The offer expires tomorrow. If the full scope is too early, I can replace it with a single [hero
> visual / product template / test clip] so you can see the result first."

Never reuse the same follow-up across prospects. The specificity is the entire mechanism.

---

## Standing rules

- Don't send a full high-ticket offer until scope, deadline, and at least a budget band are confirmed.
- Use a pilot for experimental AI, high-volume campaigns, and unfamiliar technical work.
- A call is never the only path forward, and rarely the best first one.
- Surface tool, ownership, print-production and implementation constraints before proposing.
- Answer a direct price question with a price.
- One tailored follow-up, never a generic one.
