# Platform playbooks: who is reading, and how the platform works

Read only the section for the platform in play.

This file covers the reader's state of mind and the platform's mechanics. Word counts, links,
letter structure, pricing and follow-up rules live in SKILL.md and are not repeated here.

Each platform has a different reader. Upwork, Contra and Behance job-list readers are skimming a
pile of strangers; a Behance inquiry or an email reply comes from someone who already chose to look.
Writing the same letter for all of them is why most proposals underperform.

---

## Upwork

**The reader:** a client who posted a job and got 20 to 50 applications within hours, most of them
AI-written. They are annoyed, skimming, and will decide in seconds. A confident reframe of the
client's problem is easy for an AI-written letter to produce, so on its own it does not stand out;
evidence of having looked does.

**The preview is everything.** The proposal list shows only the first ~225 characters of the cover
letter, next to the name, title, rate and badges. That fragment decides whether the proposal gets
opened. Write it last and count the characters. If it runs long, cut adjectives before substance.

**Attachments travel with the proposal.** For visual work they are the fastest proof available; use
the ATTACH block.

**Timing beats most things you can control.** Apply within the first three hours of a post going
live, ideally the first hour. After 24 hours the client has usually stopped opening new proposals;
after 48 the job is often filled and not closed. When a brief is clearly days old, say so in the
go/no-go read.

**Reading the client:** payment verified, total spend, hire rate versus posts, and the client's own
star rating all matter. A client with 100 posts and 10 hires is a connect furnace. A client rated
3.0 across 12 reviews is telling you they are difficult; 12 freelancers already said so.

**A short, plain brief is a good sign.** It usually means a real person, ready to hire, with no time
to waffle. Reply just as short (the short form); matching their energy reads as respect. A long,
polished, structured brief is often AI-generated and correlates with a client who is still shopping.

**Boosting:** in Mehmet's 12-month data, 40 boosted proposals produced threads at 22% and 100
unboosted at 20%, so boosting bought no measurable lift. Mention it only if he asks.

**Video:** a Loom (or similar) link in the proposal body is normal practice. Put it after the
observation; the video earns its click from the text.

## Fiverr

**The reader:** a buyer who posted a Brief and is receiving personalized offers from matched
sellers. Less crowded than Upwork, but the offer mechanics do a lot of the talking, so the message
has to set up the offer and not duplicate it.

**Before writing anything on Fiverr, run Step 1b** (`fiverr-qualify-quote.md`). The dominant failure
here is a good, long offer landing before the buyer has decided their own scope or budget.

**Mechanics:** Briefs stay open for a seller to answer for **72 hours**. The response is an *offer*
plus an *introduction message*. The offer carries the payment structure; pick deliberately:

- **Single payment:** one deliverable, one fee. The default.
- **Milestones:** up to 5 steps. Right for multi-phase work (concept, render, finishing), and it
  de-risks a first job with a new client.
- **Subscription:** recurring monthly. Right when the brief hints at ongoing volume (a brand that
  shoots every season, a shop adding SKUs monthly).
- **Hourly:** avoid; Mehmet prefers fixed.

Custom offers are bounded by $5 to $20,000, 1 to 90 days delivery, and 0 to 20 or unlimited
revisions.

**Links:** Fiverr's terms prohibit links that route traffic off-platform. `mehmet-turan.com` carries
no contact details; keep it to that single link, or drop it and lean on the gig gallery, which is
the safer play.

**Video:** do not link to Loom or YouTube. Upload the recording as an **attachment** to the offer.

**Scope matters more here than anywhere else.** Fiverr buyers treat the delivered file as the whole
agreement, and revision expectations are the most common source of friction. When Mehmet asks for a
scope statement, it goes into the offer description before sending.

## Behance

Two different readers. Decide which one this is before writing.

**Job-list post (cold).** Mehmet found the post in the Behance job list; the client has not seen his
work and is comparing applicants. In the tracker this is most Behance rows, and it has produced
discussions but no wins, so treat it like any cold application: the opening has to earn the read,
the proof is attached or linked Behance projects that match the job, and the first step is priced.
Check the post for a "days left to close" countdown and note it; never invent the number.

**Inquiry or message (warm).** Someone found his actual work and liked it enough to write. They have
already seen the portfolio; re-selling it wastes the goodwill. They are deciding whether the person
behind the work is someone they want to talk to, so the reply can be warmer and goes straight to
their project.

**In both cases, 35milimetre is the identity here.** Name the studio and its shape honestly: three
people, plus specialists when a job needs them; the client works with the person doing the work.

**Mechanics:** Behance freelance inquiries convert into a formal proposal, and payment runs through
Stripe or PayPal, so this behaves more like a direct client engagement than a marketplace bid. A
linked video is fine here.

## Contra

**The reader:** a client on a job board who did not pick Mehmet. Contra shows no proposal count,
client spend or hire history, and many briefs are machine-written.

**Mechanics:** applications attach up to three Work items; these do most of the proving, since his
Contra profile has no reviews yet. Conversation and payment stay on Contra. The calendar link lives
in Contra's own field. Only Mehmet submits, or Claude after he approves that exact letter in chat.

## LinkedIn / email / direct

**The reader:** someone who did not ask to hear from you, or a warm contact continuing a thread.
Either way the inbox is the competition, not other freelancers.

**The subject line is the preview.** Specific, lowercase-ish, no marketing language. It names their
thing, not yours.
Good: *"the pouch shots on your new SKUs"*, *"quick thought on the Klar bottle renders"*.
Bad: *"Post-Production Services for Your Brand"*, *"Partnership Opportunity"*.

**Assume it's read on a phone,** in a hurry, between other mail. No attachments on a first cold
email; they hurt deliverability and nobody opens them from a stranger.

**A PS line works here.** People who skim the body still read the PS, so put the single most
concrete or most human thing there (a relevant project, an offer to look at one image).

**Cold outreach honesty:** no fake familiarity, no "I've been following your work for years" unless
it's true, no invented mutual connections. The advantage is that a real person looked at their
images; that is enough.

**Output:** replace PREVIEW CHECK with the SUBJECT LINE, and offer two alternatives so Mehmet can
pick.
